CRM: why nobody believes online comparisons any more

AI avatars, top 5 lists that recommend everyone, not one verifiable figure. Why trust is eroding, and what an honest comparison has to own up to.

Indusiax30 July 20267 min read

Under a YouTube video comparing the "best CRMs of 2026", with several tens of thousands of views, the most upvoted comments hardly ever talk about the CRMs themselves. They talk about the video. "It felt like a real review video at first, then it turned into an obvious advert", writes one viewer, with sixteen upvotes. Another, with fifty-two upvotes, goes further: "The AI-generated avatar made me lose trust, I have to be honest." A third, who says he has followed the channel for a long time, admits he felt like closing the tab rather than trusting the information presented.

This is not an isolated case. It is the symptom of a wider problem: the online "CRM comparison" format has been so heavily exploited for affiliate income that it has ended up turning against itself. Buyers are no longer looking for the most complete comparison. They are looking for one they can still believe.

What happened under that video

The content in question follows a structure that has become extremely common: a presenter — or, as here, an AI-generated avatar — claims to have "worked with thousands of small businesses" before running through a ranking of the best CRMs of the moment. On paper, the promise is useful. In practice, viewers spotted the mechanism behind the promise almost immediately: the video ends up recommending, one after another, nearly all the tools tested, each with a different affiliate link.

The signal that struck commenters most was not even the substance of the content — it was its form. A synthetic presenter, with no verifiable existence, talking about personal experience with thousands of customers he could never have met. Trust was broken before the content was even judged on its merits.

Why the "top 5" format has lost its credibility

Three mechanisms explain this erosion, and they often stack up in the same piece of content.

The affiliate incentive. Content funded by commission on conversions has a structural interest in recommending widely rather than making a call. The more tools recommended, the more likely a viewer is to buy one, whichever tool actually suits their situation.

The lack of specifics. A comparison that talks about "small businesses" in general, without ever distinguishing a small service firm from an industrial SME with installed equipment and after-sales service, helps nobody decide — it reassures without informing. That is precisely what we try to avoid in our industrial vs general-purpose CRM comparison, by stating explicitly who each type of tool is right for.

Handing over to AI without saying so. Using AI to produce content is not the problem in itself — passing it off as lived human experience is what breaks trust when the viewer notices.

What an honest comparison has to own up to

A comparison does not become reliable by claiming to be neutral in a market where its author is itself a player. It becomes reliable by owning its position, and by accepting three constraints that the "top 5" format avoids precisely in order to stay vague: quoting real prices, checked on the date of publication, rather than rough ranges. Stating clearly who the recommended tool is not the right choice for, rather than staying silent about its own limits. Signing with a verifiable identity — a company, a team — rather than a voice or an avatar with no traceable existence.

A comparison that recommends everyone recommends no one. A comparison that owns up to its limits deserves to be read to the end.

Our own editorial rule, plainly stated

Indusiax sells Stator-CRM. We do not claim to be neutral on the subject, and any article that claimed to be while selling a product in the same market would be lying by omission. What we do commit to, on the other hand, can be checked: every price quoted in our comparisons is verified before publication, every article is signed by the Indusiax team — not by an individual identity invented to seem more credible — and we have never fabricated a customer testimonial or a usage statistic to back up an argument. When a competitor genuinely suits a given profile better, we say so, as in our comparison against Salesforce, HubSpot, Pipedrive and Sellsy.

It is a more modest standard than fake neutrality. It is also, judging by the reactions under the video that inspired this article, exactly what buyers are looking for today.

Why be wary of CRM comparison videos?

Many are produced with affiliate income in mind: the more widely a video recommends, the more commission it earns, whichever tool actually suits the viewer. A common sign is content that ends up recommending almost every tool tested, without ever clearly ruling one out for a given profile.

How can you recognise an honest comparison?

An honest comparison is open about who wrote it and why, quotes real, verifiable prices rather than vague ranges, and does not hesitate to say that a competitor is a better fit in certain specific cases, rather than claiming to be neutral in a market where it is itself a player.

Are the comparisons on the Indusiax blog neutral?

No, and we do not claim to be: Indusiax sells Stator-CRM. What we commit to is checking every price and every feature mentioned before publication, signing our articles as a company rather than under a fake neutral identity, and never fabricating a testimonial or a statistic.

Where can you find genuine comparisons of Stator-CRM against the competition?

Our comparison against Salesforce, HubSpot, Pipedrive and Sellsy sets out the real prices and verified functional differences, including the cases where those tools remain relevant for profiles other than ours.

Distrust of online comparisons is not going away any time soon — it has been earned by years of content written to convert rather than to inform. The best response is not to shout louder than everyone else. It is to check every figure, own your position, and let readers judge on the one thing in any piece of content that can always be verified: the facts.
