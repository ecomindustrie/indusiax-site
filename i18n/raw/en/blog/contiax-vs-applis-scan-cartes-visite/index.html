Business card scanning apps: what they do with your contacts

Today, they all read a card correctly. What sets them apart comes down to three questions: do you need an account, does it work without a network, and where does the contact end up.

Indusiax1 August 20269 min read

Ten years ago, comparing business card scanning apps made technical sense: some read poorly, mixed up phone and fax numbers, or stumbled over accented characters. Those days are gone. Automatic reading is now good everywhere, including on busy, dark or foreign-language cards. Comparing tools on their recognition rate is no longer much use.

What sets them apart lies elsewhere, and comes down to three very practical questions. Do you need to create an account? What happens when there is no signal? And where does the contact end up once it has been read? Those three answers shape real-world use far more than the quality of the extraction.

We publish one of the tools mentioned below — we may as well say so straight away. What follows compares families of approaches, not brands, because each one's features and pricing change from one quarter to the next: it is the design choices that last.

The four families of tools

Four approaches coexist on the market. They reflect different product intentions, which explains why none of them is good at everything.

One remark before going into detail: these families are not mutually exclusive. Many companies use their office suite's scanner for documents and a dedicated tool for cards. That is often the right trade-off.

The general-purpose document scanner

This is the solution you already have. The major office suites all include a scanning feature that uses the phone's camera, able to straighten a page, remove shadows and extract the text.

Its merit is real: it is already installed, already paid for, and already covered by the contract your company has signed with the publisher. From a compliance point of view, that is comfortable — the card ends up in a space whose rules you already control.

Its limit lies in what it is for. A document scanner produces an image and raw text. It does not give you back a contact: it does not know that "06" followed by eight digits is a French mobile number, that the line above is a job title, or that the word in capitals is a company name. You save on typing, not on sorting. With forty cards, that is still an evening's work.

The specialist app with an account

This is the largest family, and the oldest. These apps do exactly what you ask of them: they read the card, extract clean fields and file them in a searchable address book, often enriched and synced across devices.

The job is done well. The point to watch is structural: these tools are built around an account, because the account is the product. Your contacts form a database hosted by the publisher, which becomes the long-term custodian of business contact details you did not collect for it. Depending on the case, that database is used to sell you a subscription, to feed a company directory, or simply to keep you locked in.

This is not illegitimate, and it is not hidden: it is written in the terms of use. But it is worth reading before you scan forty prospects' cards, because responsibility for that processing remains yours. We go into this in our article on free scanning apps.

The second limit is the network. Many of these apps need to be online to process a photo. In an exhibition hall at eleven in the morning, that often means "try again later" — so the pile of paper, so Sunday evening.

The module built into a CRM

Several CRMs offer card reading directly in their mobile app. Where it exists and works, it is the shortest route: the contact is created straight away in the right place, in the tool where it will be followed up, with no export or copying.

Two caveats, however. The first is that you need to have the CRM already, and the feature is often reserved for the higher tiers. The second is more subtle: this route assumes that everyone staffing a stand has a CRM licence. At a trade show, that is almost never the case — the managing director, the applications engineer and the apprentice lending a hand do not have one. They go home with cards in their pockets, and the gap opens up again.

The no-account tool

This is the most recent approach, and the simplest to describe: a web page you open, a photo you take, a contact you get back. No installation, no sign-up, no database built up somewhere.

The benefit is not ideological, it is practical. Without an account, the tool can be lent: you hand it to whoever is on the stand with you, including someone who has no licence for your software. And since nothing is kept on the server side, the question "where are my contacts?" has only one possible answer: with you.

The honest trade-off is that a no-account tool cannot offer you an address book synced across your devices, or a searchable history of the cards from the last three years. It gives you back a contact, and it is up to you to do something with it straight away. For use at a trade show, that is exactly what is needed. For building a long-term searchable directory, it is not the right tool.

What comparisons overlook: where the contact comes from

These four families share one point that is rarely discussed: they were all designed for a rectangle of card. Yet card is no longer the main source. In a week, you receive more email signatures than you collect cards at a trade show — and those signatures carry exactly the same fields: name, job title, company, direct line, mobile, head office address.

Technically, nothing prevents you from reading them. A tool that extracts contact details from an image does not need the image to be a card: photographing the screen on which a message is displayed works, and often works better than a busy card. A signature is consistently formatted, no logo runs across the name, and it frequently gives the direct line the card leaves out.

This is a use we had not anticipated when designing Contiax: it was pointed out to us in practice, and we checked it before writing about it here. So the question to ask of whichever tool you choose is simple — does it accept a photo that is not a card, or does it impose a rigid frame that rejects everything else?

The five questions to ask before installing

Whatever tool you choose, these five questions take ten minutes of reading and save lasting regrets.

1. What happens to the photo of the card? Is it deleted after processing, or kept? If it is kept, for how long, and where?

2. Do the extracted contacts stay on a server? This is the central question. A tool that keeps nothing leaves you solely responsible — which is the normal, healthy situation.

3. What happens without a network? Outright refusal, or queuing? Test it once, in flight mode, before the trade show rather than during it.

4. How do you get the contacts out? Is there an export, in an open format, without going through a higher subscription tier? A tool you cannot get out of is not a tool, it is a holding pen.

5. Who pays, and with what? If the service is free without being offered by someone who has another reason to do so, look for the catch — there always is one.

Once the contact has been captured, the real question begins: will they get a call back? That is the subject of our article on following up after a trade show, and choosing the tool that carries that follow-up is covered in Industrial CRM or general-purpose CRM.

What is the best app for scanning a business card?

There is no single best one in absolute terms, because tools no longer differ on reading quality — it is good everywhere. They differ on three things: whether you have to create an account, whether they work without a network, and where the contact ends up. Choose according to whichever of the three bothers you most today.

Do you need to create an account to scan a business card?

Technically, no: reading a card does not require an account. When a tool insists on one, it is a product decision, not a technical constraint — the account is used to build a contact database at the publisher's end, which then becomes the custodian of contact details you did not collect for it.

Does a scanning app work offline?

It depends on the architecture. AI-based reading is done on a server, so a connection is needed at some point. The real question is what happens when there is no network: does the tool reject the photo, or queue it to be processed once the network is back? In a crowded exhibition hall, that difference decides everything.

Where do the details read from a business card go?

It varies enormously. Some tools keep a permanent copy in an account hosted on their side; others keep nothing and simply send the contact to your phone, a spreadsheet or your CRM. Since the details belong to the person who gave you their card, not to you, the question is worth asking before installing, not after.

Can you scan an email signature instead of a business card?

Yes, as long as the tool accepts any photo rather than enforcing a fixed frame. An email signature displayed on screen carries the same fields as a card — name, job title, company, phone numbers, address — and is usually easier to read: consistent formatting, no logo across the name, and often the direct line the card leaves out. Since we receive far more emails than cards, it is in practice the most plentiful source.
