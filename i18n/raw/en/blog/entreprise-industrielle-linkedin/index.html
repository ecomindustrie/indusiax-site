Why an industrial company needs to be on LinkedIn

Why an industrial SME needs a LinkedIn presence, and how to publish content from the shop floor without spending your evenings on it, thanks to Flux-Link.

Indusiax20 July 20268 min read

A machining workshop that's doing well, a team of technicians who have been repairing machines for twenty years, a job delivered on time — and not a trace of any of it online. That is the situation for most French industrial SMEs. Not for lack of expertise. For lack of time, and because LinkedIn long seemed to be the preserve of consultants and tech firms.

It's a mistake that quietly costs a lot. Customers look at a company's profile before signing a quote. The skilled technicians you are trying to recruit do the same before answering a job ad. A company that is invisible on LinkedIn comes across as one that doesn't really exist, even when its order book says otherwise.

The good news: being on LinkedIn doesn't mean becoming a community manager. All you need to do is show what is already happening in the workshop — a finished job, a new machine arriving, a troubleshooting tip. The rest is a question of tools, not writing talent.

Why industrial companies shy away from LinkedIn

Three reasons come up almost every time we discuss it with a managing director or a workshop manager.

The first is time. Between quotes, schedules, customer emergencies and managing the team, nobody has an hour to spend writing a polished post. And a sloppy post is one you would rather not publish at all.

The second is the feeling of having nothing to say. Repairing a pump, setting up a machine, delivering a part on time: it all seems ordinary when you do it every day. But what is ordinary to you is often invisible — and therefore interesting — to everyone who never sets foot in a workshop.

The third is a stubborn image: LinkedIn is supposedly the territory of consultants, start-ups and marketing directors, not of an engineering firm or a metal fabrication shop. That image is wrong, but it still holds back many industrial managers — even though the buyers, customers and candidates they are trying to win over are already on the network, every day.

What you actually miss out on by not being there

Customers check before they sign

Before awarding a job or approving a new supplier, a buyer or project manager types your company's name into a search engine. If they find a dated brochure website and no recent activity, they have doubts. If they find regular posts showing real jobs, equipment and teams at work, trust builds before the first conversation has even taken place.

Recruiting technicians increasingly happens there

Finding a qualified welder, a maintenance technician or a metal fabricator is already hard. Candidates also look at a company's profile before applying. A company that shows its day-to-day work, its machines and its successes sends a simple signal: things happen here, and we are proud to show it.

Shop-floor expertise becomes content that sets you apart

This is the most underestimated point. What your teams know how to do — diagnose a complex fault, machine a bespoke part, bring back into service a machine others had given up on — is exactly what your competitors don't show. Once highlighted, that expertise becomes a sales argument nobody else can copy.

An industrial company's strongest content isn't invented: it already exists, every day, in the workshop. You just have to show it.

The real problem isn't content, it's friction

Nobody in an industrial company is short of things to talk about. What is missing is the time to sit down, open LinkedIn, find the words, choose a photo, rephrase, publish — and do it all again the following week. That friction alone explains why so many company pages are abandoned after three posts.

In practice, what can an industrial SME publish?

There's no need for invented content or staging. What works best is what is already happening:

  • A finished job — before/after, deadline met, difficulty overcome.
  • A new machine arriving — what it lets you do that you couldn't before, or faster.
  • A job vacancy — presented with a team photo rather than a dry advert.
  • A company anniversary — ten, twenty, thirty years in business, with an old photo if you have one.
  • A piece of technical expertise — an unusual diagnosis, a fault fixed, a tip only people in the trade would spot.

What these examples have in common: none of them requires writing talent. Each can be summed up in three sentences spoken aloud just after the event, while the context is still fresh.

Making LinkedIn a habit, not a separate project

Posting on LinkedIn doesn't have to be a stand-alone task on top of everything else. For an industrial SME that already uses a CRM for sales follow-up and a field service management tool, communication becomes a natural extension of the work done every day — not an extra burden. That is the whole point of thinking in terms of a complete journey, from first customer contact to showcasing the work publicly.
