Why Indusiax uses its own software every day — and what that changes for you

Why Indusiax uses its own software day in, day out: dogfooding, data sovereignty and long-term customer trust.

Indusiax20 July 20268 min read

Because it had no choice. Indusiax uses Stator-CRM, Rotor-FSM, Vector-MOM and Flux-Link every day in its own mechanical engineering and industrial equipment business — not as a marketing line, but because these four applications were first built to solve its own problems with quotes, field service jobs, meetings and sales visibility.

The practice has a name: dogfooding, literally “eating your own dog food” — using internally what you sell. It makes a tangible difference to the quality of business software: bugs and friction are spotted by the publisher before the customer, the roadmap follows real shop-floor needs rather than marketing assumptions, and fixes arrive faster because they are first a nuisance to the people who write the code.

It is tied to a second question, less visible but just as concrete for a French industrial SME: where does your data go, and who stays in control of how it is used?

A software suite born in a workshop, not in a business plan

Indusiax did not start out as a software publisher looking for a market. It is a French mechanical engineering and industrial equipment SME that was looking for management tools suited to its trade. A CRM that understands you sell machines and service, not SaaS subscriptions. A way of keeping a record of field assessments. A place where the memory of meetings does not get lost. A way of showing off its expertise without spending its evenings on it.

None of that existed in a suitable form. The big CRMs on the market are cut out for armies of salespeople, not for a team selling industrial equipment with technical quotes and long sales cycles. Field service tools are prohibitively expensive or designed for multinationals. As for AI meeting-minutes tools, they offered to ship the most confidential recordings off to an American cloud giant.

So Indusiax built its own tools — first for itself, with no business plan and no fundraising, tested in real conditions every day in its own business. The story is told in more detail on our about page, and the wider context of this shift for industrial SMEs is developed in this article.

Dogfooding: we are the first users of our own tools

Using your own software every day changes three concrete things in a business tool.

First, how problems are detected. When a quote screen in Stator-CRM slows down data entry, or a customer follow-up goes out with the wrong information, it is not a customer who reports it first through a support ticket — it is the Indusiax team that runs into it the same day, while finalising a quote or following up a deal.

Second, the speed of the fix. An irritation that gets in the way of the internal user becomes a priority immediately, because it slows down the publisher's own business — not just that of some distant customer.

Finally, the very nature of new features. A field service report in Rotor-FSM, meeting minutes in Vector-MOM, a scheduled post in Flux-Link: every feature has had to prove its worth internally before being offered to customers.

We are neither smarter nor faster than the established publishers — we simply use our own tools every day, in our own business. When a screen is badly thought out, we are the first to run into it.

A roadmap driven by the field, not by assumptions

Many publishers build their roadmap from market studies, competitor benchmarks or one-off requests passed on by a salesperson. Indusiax starts from a different place: what is genuinely missing when you manage technical quotes, field service jobs and customer meetings yourself.

It shows in the details. A CRM that understands a deal can combine a machine sale and a service contract, rather than a pipeline designed for conventional SaaS selling. A field service tool that records an assessment without imposing an interface designed for a multinational. Meeting minutes that do not assume the subject discussed can be shipped off anywhere.

Data sovereignty: a business issue, not just a principle

For an industrial SME, data sovereignty is not an abstract debate about where servers are located. It is a question of control over information with direct commercial value.

A management committee meeting contains prices negotiated with a supplier. Workshop minutes may describe a manufacturing process. A note jotted down during an interview sometimes touches on HR matters. These are not trivial documents — they are exactly the kind of information an industrial company protects most carefully in its daily work.

Entrusting this content to a tool whose terms of use, jurisdiction or data-processing policy can change unilaterally means accepting the loss of part of your control over that information. The topic is explored in more depth for AI meeting minutes in this comparison, where the question of hosting the most sensitive recordings is tackled head-on against the American alternatives on the market.

What European hosting changes in practice

The entire Indusiax Suite hosts its customers' data in the European Union — a choice that applies to Stator-CRM, Rotor-FSM, Vector-MOM and Flux-Link, without exception.

In practice, this means that a French industrial SME's negotiated prices, processes and internal exchanges remain subject to European law rather than to a foreign jurisdiction that can change without the customer having any say. It also means that data exports remain unrestricted, and that nothing is resold.

It is a conviction we have stated from day one: the data belongs to the customer, not to the publisher that hosts it.

We take the principle as far as our free tools. Contiax, our free business card scanner, keeps neither the image nor the extracted contact details: the contact goes back into the user's phone, and nothing stays on our servers. The full journey of a scanned card, and the questions to ask any publisher before entrusting it with contacts, are in our article on scanning apps and the GDPR.

Trust that outlasts a sale

These two threads — dogfooding and sovereignty — meet on the same ground: the length of the relationship.

A publisher that lives with its own irritations fixes them faster, because it suffers them before its customer does. A company that stays in control of its data has fewer reasons to leave, because it is not held hostage by an impossible export or a jurisdiction that changes the rules along the way. Together, these two principles reduce the reasons to cancel and build a relationship of trust that lasts over time, rather than a one-off sale.

It is exactly the kind of relationship an industrial SME already has with its own customers: the ones who keep coming back for years, not the ones who buy once and disappear. Where is Indusiax data hosted?

The entire Indusiax Suite — Stator-CRM, Rotor-FSM, Vector-MOM and Flux-Link — hosts its customers' data in the European Union.

Why does data sovereignty matter for an industrial SME?

Because an industrial company's meetings and documents contain sensitive information — negotiated prices, manufacturing processes, sometimes HR matters. European hosting limits the risks linked to terms of use or a jurisdiction that can change unilaterally.

What does dogfooding mean in practice?

It means a software publisher using its own software every day in its own business. At Indusiax, that means the company's quotes, service reports, meeting minutes and LinkedIn posts all go through the same tools that are sold to customers.

Does Indusiax sell or share customer data?

No. The data belongs to the customer, exports remain unrestricted, and nothing is resold.

Indusiax does not use its own software to illustrate a slogan. It is the other way round: the software exists because it first had to work for the company itself, quote after quote, job after job, meeting after meeting. That daily demand, combined with European hosting that leaves control of the data with those who produce it, is what builds a lasting relationship rather than a one-off sale.
