From first contact to customer report: how Stator, Rotor, Vector and Flux work together

How Stator, Rotor, Vector and Flux connect from the first quote to the LinkedIn post, with no re-keying and no information lost along the way.

Indusiax20 July 20268 min read

A quote in one piece of software. The minutes of the technical meeting in a notebook, or nowhere at all. The service report sent as a PDF by email, lost in an inbox three months later. Once the job is finished, nobody mentions it on LinkedIn because nobody has time to write a post. That is daily life in many industrial SMEs: each stage of a deal lives in its own corner.

An integrated software suite for industry addresses a specific problem: moving information from one stage to the next without anyone having to type it in again. The sales rep who won the deal knows what the technician found on site. The technician sees the machine's full history before starting work. And the finished job becomes, in three dictated sentences, proof of expertise published on LinkedIn.

This article follows the typical journey of a deal, from the first sales contact through to the communication that comes out of it, to show in concrete terms what connecting the tools changes — and what it doesn't change if you would rather start small.

The first sales contact: the quote goes out from Stator-CRM

It usually all starts with a phone call, an email, or a request passed on by an existing customer. In Stator-CRM, that request becomes a customer record if one doesn't already exist, then a deal. The sales rep adds the stated requirements, prices a quote and sends it as a PDF. Nothing out of the ordinary so far — that's the basic job of an industrial CRM: centralising customers, quotes and the automatic follow-ups that stop you forgetting to call back.

What matters at this stage is that this customer record will become the anchor point for everything that follows. Each later step — the technical meeting, the intervention, the report — will be attached to it instead of ending up in a separate file.

The technical meeting: Vector-MOM structures the minutes

The customer is interested in the quote, but a technical discussion is often needed before signing: clarifying an access constraint, confirming a part number, understanding a requirement that was poorly explained over the phone. That meeting, on site or by video call, is usually forgotten — nobody takes usable notes, or the notes stay in a notebook only their author can read.

Vector-MOM records the discussion and automatically produces structured minutes: who said what, the technical points raised, the decisions taken. No need to write it all up by hand when you get home in the evening. And because Vector is part of the same suite as Stator, the minutes go straight into the relevant customer record — no copying and pasting, no email to send round to keep the team informed.

The practical result: the next person who opens the customer record in Stator — a sales colleague, the managing director wanting a quick update, or later the technician who will do the job — sees exactly what was said in that discussion. Without having been there, without having to ask.

The signed quote: the intervention is scheduled and carried out with Rotor-FSM

The quote has been accepted. Now the on-site intervention needs scheduling. This is where Rotor-FSM comes in: scheduling technicians, an assessment form filled in on a tablet during the intervention, photos taken on site, and a PDF report generated automatically and handed to the customer at the end of the visit.

What the technician already knows on arrival

Thanks to the connection with Stator, the technician taking on the job doesn't arrive empty-handed. They have access to the context of the deal — what was discussed beforehand, exactly what the customer expects. They don't have to call the sales rep to understand why they are there, or ask again for information the customer has already given once.

What comes back after the intervention

Once the assessment form is complete and the report generated, the intervention history joins the installed base in Stator: the machine concerned, the type of intervention, the photos, the report. The next time this customer calls — about a breakdown, a maintenance quote or just a question — anyone on the sales team can look up the machine's full history without picking up the phone to ask the technician, who may well be on another job.

What connecting the tools changes is not the quality of each task on its own — it's what happens between them: nobody re-keys, nobody asks again, nothing gets lost in a stray email.

The finished job becomes a LinkedIn post with Flux-Link

The intervention went well and the customer is happy, but this expertise usually stays invisible beyond the customer concerned. Few industrial SMEs have the time or the habit of communicating about their jobs — writing a post takes time, and it isn't anyone's job in the technical team.

Flux-Link reduces the task to three dictated sentences and a few photos of the job — the very ones taken during the intervention with Rotor. The AI writes a professional post and publishes it on LinkedIn. The technician hasn't written anything, the managing director hasn't had to proofread anything long, and the company showcases work that would otherwise have gone unseen.

This is where the loop closes: the post catches the attention of prospects who would never otherwise have seen the job. Some of them will come to Stator-CRM as a new first contact — and the cycle starts again.

What siloed tools lose along the way

Without a connection between the tools, each of these stages still exists — but in isolation. The quote in a spreadsheet or a generic CRM. The meeting minutes nowhere, or in the personal notes of whoever took them. The service report sent as a PDF by email and never attached to a customer record anyone can consult. The LinkedIn post, if there is one, written from memory weeks later, without the technical details that would have made it credible.

The cost of this sprawl doesn't show up line by line — it shows up in time wasted looking for information, in questions asked again of a customer who has already answered once, in subscriptions to tools that don't talk to each other. Do you have to adopt everything at once?

No. Nothing obliges you to start with all four tools. Many industrial SMEs start with one specific need — a missing CRM, poorly tracked field interventions, minutes that go astray — and add the other tools gradually, as the need arises. The connection between the tools is an advantage once several are in place, not an obligation from day one. Each tool in the suite works on its own, with its own price, set out in detail on the pricing page.

Do you have to subscribe to all 4 tools at once?

No. Each tool — Stator, Rotor, Vector, Flux — works on its own and can be adopted separately. The full suite becomes worthwhile when several parts of the business need to be connected, but nothing stops you starting with a single tool and adding the others later.

Is data really shared between the tools?

Yes, with the full suite. Customer records are shared by Stator and Rotor, Vector's minutes go automatically into the Stator customer record, and a single login gives access to everything. Used separately, each tool remains independent and shares nothing with the others.

How much does the full suite cost?

The full suite, with all 4 tools connected, costs €990 per user per year, with 2 months free. Each tool bought separately has its own price, starting at €120 per year — or €12 per month — for Vector-MOM or Flux-Link. Full details are on the pricing page.

Which tool should you start with?

It depends on the biggest point of friction in your business today. If sales follow-up and quotes are the problem, start with Stator-CRM. If it's field interventions and customer reports, start with Rotor-FSM. Vector-MOM and Flux-Link meet more occasional needs — minutes and communication — and are easy to add later.

An industrial deal rarely passes through just one tool: it starts with a quote, goes through a meeting, takes shape in the field and may end up as communication. Whether you start with a single piece of software or the full suite, the essentials stay the same — less re-keying, less lost information, and a team that knows what the others have seen without having to ask them.
