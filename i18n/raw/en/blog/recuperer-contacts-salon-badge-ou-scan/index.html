Badge scanner, app, manual entry: the real cost of a trade show contact

Four ways to capture a contact on a stand, compared on the only two criteria that decide the outcome: how soon you can call back, and where the data ends up.

Indusiax31 July 20269 min read

When you exhibit at an industrial trade show, the question of how to capture contacts always comes up too late — usually while filling in the organiser's form, somewhere between choosing the carpet and deciding how many exhibitor badges you need. You tick the "badge scanner" box because it is there, or you leave it blank and decide to "do it the old way". Either way, nobody has compared the options.

Yet there are four ways to capture a contact on a stand, and price is not what sets them apart. They differ on two things: how long before you can call back, and where the data ends up. Everything else — recognition quality, convenience, how good the kit looks — is secondary.

This article gives an honest comparison of the badge scanner rented from the organiser, manual entry once you are back, consumer scanning apps, and scanning straight into your own tools. Each has situations where it is the right choice. None is right everywhere.

One useful point before going into detail: these methods are not mutually exclusive. Many exhibitors scan badges for volume and photograph the cards of serious contacts. That is often the most sensible combination.

The badge scanner: what you are really paying for

Let us start with what it does well, because it does do some things well. The badge scanner is fast, unobtrusive and comprehensive: one movement and the visitor is recorded, even if they have no card on them — which happens more and more often. On a busy stand seeing two hundred people a day, it is the only option that keeps up.

But you need to know what you are buying. First, the data is not from your conversation; it is from the visitor's registration form. Many visitors register with a generic company address, an approximate job title and no direct number. You get an administrative identity, not the mobile number of the maintenance manager you have just spent twenty minutes talking to.

Next, the delay. It is the most consistent criticism of the system, and a very concrete one. A company specialising in trade show lead capture puts it like this: "contacts are locked in a portal, the CSV file isn't there, the enrichment isn't there. By the time the data finally reaches your CRM, it's three days later and the conversation has already gone cold." And further on, more bluntly: "a CSV file that arrives five days after the show slows you down." When the useful window is forty-eight hours — we explain why in our article on following up after a trade show — a file delivered on day five arrives after the battle is over.

Finally, dependence. The hardware is rented for the duration of the show, the contacts live on a portal you access with temporary credentials, and the export comes in whatever format you are given. You do not own the chain. For one show a year, that is acceptable; for an exhibitor doing four or five regional shows, the bill and the dependence pile up.

Manual entry: the hidden cost of Sunday evening

The most widespread method is still the simplest: you collect the cards, bring them back and type them in. It has a real merit — the data is with you, immediately, with no intermediary and no subscription — and it does not depend on any technology that might fail in an exhibition hall.

Its cost lies elsewhere. Allow one to two minutes per card for clean entry: read, type, check the phone number format, check the spelling of the email address. Forty cards is a good hour, and more if you add a line of context for each. That hour is perfectly doable — the problem is when.

Because it never gets done that same evening. It gets done on Sunday, or the following Tuesday, or not at all. And by then, two things have happened: the forty-eight-hour window has closed, and the context of each conversation has faded. You end up entering forty clean, anonymous lines, with no idea which ones deserved a call. Manual entry does not fail on quality; it fails on timing.

Scanning apps: the right idea, and the catch

Photographing the card instead of copying it out is the right idea. Automatic business card recognition is now very reliable: name, company, job title, phone numbers and email are extracted correctly in the vast majority of cases, even on busy cards or cards in a foreign language. It takes two seconds, standing up, on the stand, between two visitors. That is exactly where it should be done.

The catch is not technical; it is economic. A service that gives you something genuinely useful without ever charging you must be making money some other way — and you need to know which. The most common models: a mandatory account that turns your contacts into a database hosted by the publisher, a free version capped at a handful of cards to sell the subscription, advertising, or, more discreetly, commercial use of the contact details collected.

This point deserves serious attention, because the data in question is not yours: it is the business contact details of a person who handed their card to you, not to the whole world. We devote an entire article to this question — a free scanning app: where do your contacts end up? — with the five questions to ask before installing anything. And if you would rather compare families of tools than capture methods, that is the subject of our comparison of business card scanning apps.

A side benefit that is often overlooked: a tool that reads contact details from an image is not limited to card stock. An email signature, photographed on the computer screen, is read in exactly the same way — and actually reads better, as there is no logo cutting across the name. The trade show is therefore just the peak use of a habit that serves all year round, every time someone writes to you for the first time.

The other practical limitation is the network. In a large hall, mid-morning, the connection is often saturated. An app that has to be online to read a card sends you straight back to where you started: the pile of paper, to be dealt with later.

The two criteria that really decide

If you boil the choice down to the essentials, two axes are enough to place the four methods — and to understand why some combinations are uncomfortable.

Horizontal axis: when can you call back? This is the criterion that decides your conversion rate, because it determines whether your message arrives while the prospect still remembers you. A contact available on the stand allows a message that same evening. A contact delivered on day five lands in an inbox where four competitors have already been.

Vertical axis: where does the data end up? This is not an ideological question; it is a question of dependence and responsibility. Data that lives on an organiser's portal or in a publisher's account needs exporting before it can be used, disappears if the service shuts down, and makes you responsible for processing whose storage you do not control.

A contact that arrives five days after the show is not a late contact. It is a contact competing with everyone who got there first.

How to choose for your trade show

There is no single answer, but there are clear-cut cases.

Very high-footfall show, consumer-facing stand, volume objective. The badge scanner holds its own here: it records everyone without slowing the flow, which no other method can do. Just plan for the file arriving after the show and needing some rework. And keep a second channel for serious contacts, who deserve better than a registration line.

Regional technical show, thirty to fifty contacts, ten of them genuinely qualified. This is the situation of the vast majority of French industrial SMEs, and the one where a rented scanner pays off least. The right combination is simple: photograph each card on the stand, add a line of context, sort them into three categories before you leave. In the evening, all that is left is to write.

You exhibit several times a year. This is where ownership becomes decisive. If your contacts pile up each year on a different portal, you are not building a database: you are renting the same service four times. A chain that runs from the card to the CRM, and stays the same from one show to the next, builds value over time. Last year's contacts become this year's follow-ups.

It is free, with no account to create and no app to install, because its main purpose is to make our suite known: it is our own business card.

Should you rent the badge scanner offered by the trade show organiser?

It is worthwhile if you expect very heavy footfall and are prepared to receive your contacts later. Its two limitations are well known: the data often arrives as a file delivered after the show, and it is the data from the visitor's registration form, not from your conversation. For an industrial SME with thirty or forty serious contacts, an immediate solution usually pays off better.

Does a badge scan replace a business card?

No, it is not the same data. The badge returns what the visitor told the organiser when registering: often a generic email address and an approximate job title. The business card carries the direct contact details the person chooses to give you, including their mobile number. The two complement each other, but only the card is the contact you will actually call back.

How long does manual entry of business cards take after a trade show?

Allow one to two minutes per card for clean, checked data entry, so a good hour for forty cards, and more if you add the context of each conversation. The real cost is not that hour: it is the fact that it almost always gets put off, which pushes the follow-up beyond the useful forty-eight-hour window.

Is a free business card scanning app reliable?

Recognition quality is now excellent on most tools. The real question is the business model: a free service that requires an account, shows advertising or keeps your contacts earns its money one way or another. So you need to look at what happens to the data, not just at the price tag.

Choosing a lead capture method looks like a logistics question. It is one for the duration of the show; then it becomes a sales question: the method you choose determines when you can call back, with what information, and whether you will still have those contacts next year. Three questions to ask yourself before ticking a box on an exhibitor form — not afterwards.
