Excel or CRM? The real test for a small industrial team

A good spreadsheet is sometimes all you need. Here is how to tell whether your team really needs a CRM, or is about to pay for a tool it will only half use.

Indusiax30 July 20268 min read

On a professional forum dedicated to CRM, a small company of eleven people recently described what it needed: to centralise contacts, track a few deals and keep notes on each customer. The question was simple: which software should we choose? The most upvoted answer was just as simple, and ran against what you would expect from a community of CRM experts: "I don't think you need a CRM yet. A well-structured spreadsheet can handle contacts, notes, deal tracking and weekly reporting for a team of eleven."

This is not an isolated accident. It is a pattern that comes up again and again as soon as you look at what small businesses actually ask for: most of them only need three things. Not a system. Three things.

We sell a CRM. It might be in our interest to convince you that you need one today. We would rather help you answer the question honestly — because a customer who buys an oversized tool too early is, statistically, a customer who abandons it within the year. Here is how to decide.

The three-function test

Almost all requests from small teams on this kind of forum boil down to three needs: centralised contacts, basic deal tracking, and notes attached to each customer. Nothing else. No lead scoring, no automated marketing sequences, no multi-level dashboards.

If that is exactly your situation — a handful of people, a reasonable number of customers, nobody who needs advanced automation — the question is not "which CRM should I choose" but "do I really need a CRM". A shared spreadsheet, with a contacts tab, a tracking tab and a clear naming convention, covers that scope with no subscription at all.

When a spreadsheet really does the job

There is nothing shameful about running your sales activity from a spreadsheet when the team is small and the need is simple. It is often the most rational choice: zero cost, zero learning curve, zero dependence on a vendor. The most upvoted comment in that same thread puts it bluntly: a well-kept spreadsheet can cover contacts, notes, deal tracking and weekly reporting for a small team.

The real risk is not staying on Excel too long. It is jumping straight to an oversized general-purpose CRM at the first frustration, without having identified what is actually missing. We look at this precise trap, and why it kills adoption within a few months, in our article on CRMs that don't survive their first year.

The three signals that you are reaching the limit

Three signs reliably indicate that a spreadsheet has reached its limit — and that a dedicated tool is becoming worthwhile rather than superfluous.

Version conflicts. As soon as several people edit the same file, versions diverge: one sales rep works on a local copy, another overwrites an update by mistake. Nobody knows any more which version is the right one.

Follow-ups that depend on memory. A spreadsheet reminds nobody of anything. As long as one person is following a few dozen contacts, they remember. As soon as the volume grows or several people have to follow up, quotes die in silence — a problem we covered in depth in our article on customer follow-up.

No overview. The managing director has to ask someone to compile the figures to know where sales stand, instead of having them in front of them in real time.

The hidden cost that never appears on the invoice

A spreadsheet is free to buy, but rarely free to use beyond a certain threshold. The time lost consolidating versions, finding out who changed what, rebuilding a customer history scattered across several files: that time appears on no invoice, but it is real. It is paid for in forgotten deals and hours of manual reporting.

That is the tipping point you need to be able to spot — not too early, so you don't pay for a tool you won't use, and not too late, so you don't lose sales through disorganisation.

The right time to change tools is not when you can afford it. It is when the spreadsheet starts to cost more, in lost time, than a subscription.

What a right-sized CRM should add, not take away

The counter-example to avoid is not staying on Excel — it is migrating to a tool designed for sales teams of several dozen people, with marketing automation and SaaS-startup vocabulary that has nothing to do with what an industrial SME sells. A good transition tool should keep the simplicity of a spreadsheet — few fields, nothing superfluous — while precisely addressing the three signals above: a single version of the truth, follow-ups that go out without anyone having to think about them, an overview available at any time. Does a small company really need a CRM?

Not always, and not straight away. If a single person manages the contacts, the volume stays low and there are no systematic follow-ups to orchestrate, a well-structured spreadsheet can be enough for a while. The need becomes real as soon as several people edit the same data, or follow-ups have to go out without depending on someone's memory.

What are the signs that you have outgrown Excel?

Three signals come up most often: versions of the file that diverge between colleagues, follow-ups or quotes that are forgotten for lack of an automatic reminder, and a managing director who no longer has an overview without asking someone to compile the figures.

The right tool is not the most powerful one; it is the one that matches the size of the problem exactly.
