Industrial CRM or general-purpose CRM? What really changes for an industrial SME

Technical quotes, installed equipment, after-sales service, geolocated field rounds: why a real industrial CRM looks nothing like Salesforce or HubSpot.

Indusiax20 July 20268 min read

A salesperson selling industrial equipment does not do the same job as a salesperson selling a SaaS subscription. Yet most industrial SMEs still run their sales activity with tools designed for the second job. The answer fits in one sentence: an industrial CRM is not a simplified version of a general-purpose CRM, it is a tool built around different business objects — the technical quote, the installed equipment base, after-sales service, the field round.

Salesforce, HubSpot and Pipedrive are not bad tools. They are in fact excellent in their own field: large sales teams, standardised sales cycles, marketing automation, lead management at scale. The problem is not their quality. It is their playing field, which is simply not that of an SME that sells and maintains industrial equipment.

This article compares the two approaches structurally, without running anyone down, to help you choose using the right criteria.

What general-purpose CRMs do very well

Let us be honest: Salesforce and HubSpot dominate their market for good reasons. They excel for organisations with dozens or even hundreds of salespeople, repetitive sales cycles and a strong need for marketing automation — lead scoring, email campaigns, nurturing sequences. Their vocabulary ("lead", "pipeline", "deal", "stage") fits software sales, financial services or standardised services perfectly.

Their ecosystem of modules is huge, their integrations cover almost every tool on the market, and their reporting power is real. For a SaaS team or a large, general-purpose sales department, they are often sound choices.

The problem: an industrial quote is not a "deal"

In a general-purpose CRM, a sales opportunity boils down to an amount, a closing probability and a date. That is enough when you sell a fixed-price subscription. It is no longer enough when you sell a made-to-order machine, a technical spare part or a service with several configuration options.

An industrial technical quote needs detailed lines, product references, specifications, manufacturing lead times and often several successive versions before the customer signs off. Squeezing all that into a free-text field or a generic quoting module means spending your time working around the tool rather than selling. That is why Stator-CRM has built-in generation of technical PDF quotes, designed for this line of work — not as an add-on bolted onto a generic pipeline.

Installed equipment: the data Salesforce cannot track

This is probably the most fundamental difference. In industrial sales, the customer relationship does not end at signature. Every machine sold stays installed at the customer's site, with a history, maintenance and a service life — and potentially future sales of spare parts or maintenance contracts.

A general-purpose CRM simply has no "machine installed at the customer's site" object. You can cobble something together with custom fields or notes, but it remains fragile and nobody on the team has a reliable view of the equipment actually in place. A CRM designed for industry, on the other hand, links each machine natively to the customer record: installation date, past interventions, parts used. That is what turns a customer record into a genuine operational history rather than a mere address book.

After-sales and sales: two jobs that must share the same customer record

In many industrial SMEs, after-sales and sales work with two different tools, or even two different spreadsheets. The result: the salesperson does not know that a machine keeps breaking down at a customer's site, and the technician does not know that a replacement quote is in progress. This lack of communication is expensive — in missed sales and in unhappy customers alike.

That is exactly the link the Indusiax Suite covers: Stator-CRM handles the customer relationship and quotes, Rotor-FSM manages field service jobs and machine assessments, and the two meet on the same customer record. A general-purpose CRM asks you to rephrase your business in its vocabulary. An industrial CRM already speaks yours — quotes, machines, rounds, after-sales.

Sales rounds and field work: the missing geolocation

An industrial salesperson does not work remotely from an office with calls and video meetings. He drives, visits workshops, plans rounds across a territory. General-purpose CRMs sometimes offer a map, but it often remains a secondary module, designed to visualise accounts rather than to organise a real day in the field.

Stator-CRM makes mapping and sales rounds a core function: see your customers on a map, organise your day by area, record a visit report straight from the field. It is not a gimmick — it is the daily routine of a salesperson selling industrial equipment.

The real cost: price, modules and learning curve

General-purpose CRMs often advertise an attractive entry price, then the cost climbs with the modules needed to get close to real requirements — advanced reporting, automation, integrations. For an industrial SME that needs neither marketing automation nor sophisticated lead scoring, much of that power goes unused, while still being paid for.

The learning curve weighs too. A general-purpose tool often needs weeks of configuration before it fits the business — if it ever does. A CRM already structured around industry's business objects cuts that start-up time, since there is no translating to do between the tool's vocabulary and that of the trade. How to choose: the practical criteria

Rather than deciding on a vendor's reputation, ask yourself these practical questions:

Do you sell equipment or technical services with detailed quotes? If so, check that the tool natively handles technical quote lines and not just an overall amount.

Do your customers keep installed equipment over the long term? If so, an "installed equipment" object linked to the customer record is not a luxury, it is a necessity.

Do your after-sales and sales teams need to share the same information? If so, look for a suite where CRM and field service management communicate natively, rather than two sealed-off tools.

Are your salespeople in the field more than in the office? If so, mapping and rounds must be at the heart of the tool, not a paid extra.

If you answer yes to several of these questions, a general-purpose CRM will make you waste time working around its limits. An industrial CRM such as Stator-CRM already starts from the right place.
