Alternatives to Praxedo: the checklist for choosing without regret

Praxedo is a solid reference on the French market. Teams that look elsewhere rarely do so for reasons of quality — here are the real reasons, and the checklist for deciding.

Indusiax1 August 20269 min read

Let us start with what is fair: Praxedo is an established reference on the French field service management market. Broad functional coverage, mature scheduling, years of field experience, a large installed base. Companies that look for an alternative almost never do so because the product is bad.

They do so because of fit. A tool designed to manage thirty technicians on the road does not behave like a tool for four — not in its price, not in the time it takes to configure, not in what it expects of the user. It is a question of size and scope, not quality.

This article does not rank software from best to worst: no such ranking exists. It gives you the checklist for deciding in your own case, and it says honestly where we stand — we publish one of the tools mentioned.

Why people look for an alternative

Four reasons keep coming up, and none of them is a complaint about quality.

Cost relative to headcount. Field service software is generally billed per user per month. The model makes sense, but it inevitably penalises small teams: with three technicians, the cost per job becomes high, because the value of a scheduling tool grows with the number of people to coordinate. A four-person company does not have a coordination problem — it has a traceability problem.

The scope actually used. Many teams discover, two years in, that they use three screens out of fifteen. Paying for a comprehensive tool is only a waste if complexity has a cost — and it does: configuration time, training new starters, and above all the daily friction for the technician.

The commitment. Multi-year contracts are normal in business software, but they assume you got it right when you signed. For a company going digital for the first time, committing for three years before understanding its own needs is a gamble.

The burden of getting started. The richer a tool is, the more its roll-out ties up someone in-house. In an SME where the maintenance manager is also the person doing the jobs, that someone does not exist.

What really needs comparing

The comparison grids published online line up dozens of features. That is a convenient way to fill a table, and a poor way to decide. Six criteria are enough, and they can be tested.

Adoption by the technician. This is the criterion that decides everything, and you will not find it on a product sheet. Have each candidate tried out by the technician who is least comfortable with a screen. If they complete their first report without help, you have your answer. A comprehensive tool the team works around is worth less than a simple tool it fills in.

Recurring due dates. Many tools treat a job as an isolated event: an appointment, a write-up. If your business involves periodic inspections attached to equipment, check that the tool can hold a due date and flag it before it falls due. Otherwise you will keep a spreadsheet running alongside it, and the benefit evaporates.

The report handed to the customer. A service report is as much a commercial document as a technical one. Check whether it carries your branding, whether it can be configured, and whether it goes to the customer without any manual handling.

Offline operation. Basement boiler room, plant room, remote site: the network drops out exactly where the work is done. A tool that needs a connection to open a form sends the technician back to paper.

Getting your data out. In what format can you retrieve the equipment history and the reports? Is it included? Ask before signing — afterwards, the question has no negotiating value.

The cost for your actual headcount. Not the advertised price per user, but the annual bill for the exact number of people who will use the tool, options included.

The options on the French market

The French-speaking market falls into four families, plus a fifth option people forget to mention.

Comprehensive field service management suites, of which Praxedo is the best-known example. Scheduling, route optimisation, customer portal, integrations. The right choice when coordinating many technicians is the real problem.

Business management tools for service SMEs, such as Organilog, which cover field jobs but also quotes, invoicing and time tracking. Relevant when the company wants a single tool and accepts that it will be average everywhere rather than excellent somewhere.

Mobile form builders, such as Kizeo Forms. These are not field service software: they are tools that replace paper with a form on a mobile device, and they do that very well. They are flexible and inexpensive, but they hold no equipment base, no due dates and no per-machine history — you have to build that yourself.

Trade-specific tools, of which Rotor-FSM is one, focused on statutory inspection and equipment traceability rather than route optimisation.

And the status quo. Sticking with paper is a defensible option if your volume is low and your reports are rarely requested again. It stops being one the day a customer, an insurer or an inspector asks for proof of a past job. We put figures on that tipping point in our article on field service management software.

This is the variable that best explains successful choices and regrets.

The most useful reading of this table is the middle row. Between two and twenty technicians, the classic mistake is to buy a tool sized for coordination when the problem is traceability. You end up paying for route scheduling, and still searching for a report in an inbox.

The three pitfalls of switching

Switching tools always costs more than the price difference. Three pitfalls keep coming up.

Migrating the history. Past reports and equipment records do not transfer in one click. Decide early what you really need to migrate: often the equipment base and the latest due dates are enough, and the history remains available in the old tool for the rest of the contract.

Switching everything at once. Moving the whole team over on a Monday morning is the surest way to provoke rejection. One willing technician, two weeks, one type of job: that is enough to discover what the demo did not show.

Exhaustive configuration before go-live. Trying to describe the entire equipment base before the first job delays the start by six months. Begin with the twenty most frequently inspected items; the rest gets added visit by visit.

The best field service software is not the most comprehensive. It is the one the technician fills in without having to be asked twice.

Where we stand, plainly

We publish Rotor-FSM, and it does not suit everyone. It is designed for companies whose business is inspecting and checking an equipment base: periodic inspections, configurable machine records, PDF reports with your branding, due-date tracking. It does not do route optimisation, and is not meant to.

If your problem is placing twenty-five technicians on a map while minimising mileage, a comprehensive suite will do better, and Praxedo is a good candidate. If your problem is knowing which fire extinguisher falls due next month and finding the report from two years ago in ten seconds, that is our territory.

Is Praxedo bad software?

No, and it would be dishonest to suggest so. Praxedo is an established reference on the French field service management market, with broad functional coverage and years of field experience behind it. Teams that look for an alternative almost always do so because of fit — size, budget, the scope they actually use — not quality.

What is the main criterion when choosing field service software?

How far technicians actually adopt it, well ahead of the breadth of features. Comprehensive software that technicians work around delivers less value than a simple tool they genuinely fill in. Before comparing feature lists, have two or three candidates tried out by the technician who is least comfortable with computers.

Can you get your data back when you change field service software?

That depends entirely on the contract and the export format offered. It is the question to ask before signing, not when you leave. Always ask: in what format can service reports and the equipment history be exported, and is that export included or charged for?

Does field service software handle statutory inspections?

Not all of it does. Many tools treat a job as a one-off event — an appointment, a report — with no notion of a recurring due date attached to a piece of equipment. If your business involves periodic inspections such as the French VGP (vérifications générales périodiques — France's mandatory periodic inspections), this is a make-or-break criterion to check first, because working around it by hand cancels out much of the benefit.
