Which software should an industrial SME choose to go digital in 2026?

CRM, field work, meetings, LinkedIn: the complete comparison of software for taking an industrial SME digital in 2026, category by category.

Indusiax20 July 20267 min read

You don't need a €10,000 audit to answer this question: an industrial SME that wants to go digital in 2026 needs four software building blocks, and not one more. A tool to manage customers and quotes, a tool to run field interventions, a tool to stop losing track of meetings, and a tool to have an online presence without spending your evenings on it.

The real question isn't “which software should I choose” but “generic or industry-specific”. Mainstream CRMs and tools work for any business — and that is precisely their problem: they don't speak the language of a machine shop, an inspection body or an industrial maintenance company. Industry-specific tools, on the other hand, are built around your actual processes: technical quotes, machine assessment forms, VGP inspections, sales rounds.

This comparison reviews each category of need, compares the generic approach with the industry-specific approach, and helps you choose according to the size and maturity of your company.

What does an industrial SME really need to go digital?

Before comparing software, list your needs. An industrial SME — engineering, maintenance, regulatory inspection, equipment distribution — broadly handles four flows: customer relations (quotes, follow-ups, history), field work (interventions, technical assessments, reports), internal information (meetings, decisions, follow-through) and external communication (visibility, recruitment, brand image).

The classic trap is stacking generic tools for each building block: a generic CRM here, a spreadsheet there, a note-taking app somewhere else, an agency for LinkedIn. Taken one by one, each tool seems reasonable. Added together, they are expensive, they don't talk to each other, and nobody uses them to the full. We go into this in detail in our article on the real cost of software sprawl — the figure often surprises managing directors who had never done the maths.

Industrial CRM or generic CRM: which one for selling equipment and services?

CRMs such as Salesforce, HubSpot or Pipedrive were designed for sales teams handling generic “leads” and “pipelines” — SaaS software, financial services, property. Nothing in their vocabulary or their fields matches the sale of industrial equipment: no structured technical quote, no tracking of the installed base, no sales round with customer mapping.

Stator-CRM starts from the opposite problem: it was designed for industrial sales, not adapted after the fact. Customer records with the installed machine base, PDF quotes generated in a few clicks, automatic follow-ups so no deal gets lost in limbo, sales rounds with customer mapping, built-in after-sales service, and AI assistance to find information without digging. The details are on the Stator-CRM page.

For an SME that sells and services equipment, the gain isn't just time saved on data entry — it's a CRM that sales reps and technicians actually use, because it fits their trade instead of imposing a foreign vocabulary on them.

How do you digitise field interventions and machine assessments?

Providers of regulatory inspections (VGP — France's mandatory periodic inspections of lifting and handling equipment), maintenance companies and inspection bodies have a problem that standard CRMs and ticketing tools don't solve: filling in an assessment form on site, with photos, and producing a professional PDF report to hand to the customer the same day.

That is what Rotor-FSM does: intervention scheduling, assessment forms filled in on a tablet directly in the field, embedded photos, automatic PDF report generation, and a complete history for each machine — useful for proving compliance or tracing a piece of equipment's wear over time. Available at €390/user/year, with 2 months free — on your own or as a team, the price simply follows your headcount.

A spreadsheet or a generic CRM can just about pass muster here. But as soon as the volume of interventions rises, the lack of structure — no machine history, no automatic PDF generation — turns into hours lost every week reformatting reports by hand.

Generic software forces you into its mould; industry-specific software fits yours.

Automating meeting minutes without sending your data to the United States

Transcription and automatic minute-taking tools have multiplied in recent years — most of them hosted on American clouds, which raises a real question for an industrial company that discusses drawings, prices, suppliers or processes in its meetings.

Vector-MOM does the same job — recording, transcription, speaker identification, structured drafting of the minutes — but with an argument few competing tools can claim: European hosting and confidentiality. At €12/user/month, it is one of the cheapest investments in this comparison, with time saved immediately after every meeting.

The best way to judge whether industry-specific software suits you is to try it on your own files — not on a generic demo.

Being visible on LinkedIn when you have neither the time nor the taste for community management

Digital communication remains the poor relation of digitalisation in industrial SMEs: not for lack of interest, but for lack of time and writing skills. A managing director or a technician coming back from a job site has neither the energy nor the inclination to write a polished LinkedIn post.

Flux-Link solves this problem differently from an agency or a standard scheduling tool: the user dictates three sentences, adds a few photos from the job site, and the AI writes and publishes a professional post. No editorial calendar to keep up, no brief to send to a contractor. €12/user/month, the same price as Vector-MOM.

Should you choose an integrated suite or separate tools?
