Forty cards in your pocket: why half of them will never get a call

The show went well. Three weeks later, the pile is still on the desk. This is not a question of sales motivation — it is a mechanical gap between the conversation and the file.

Indusiax31 July 20268 min read

A trade show is expensive: the stand, the hotel nights, three days during which the workshop runs without you. And it usually produces just one tangible thing: a pile of business cards. That pile is supposed to turn into quotes. In the day-to-day reality of many small and mid-sized industrial companies, it turns into a stack of paper that migrates from the bag to the desk, from the desk to the drawer, and from the drawer to the bin six months later.

This is not isolated carelessness: it is the most widely discussed friction point on the subject. On LinkedIn this month, one practitioner in the sector put it bluntly — "most trade show contacts die in a pile of business cards; not because salespeople don't care, but because there is a gap between the conversation and the CRM". Another is even more direct: "'we'll follow them up after the show' is where deals go to die".

The good news is that the problem is neither commercial nor human. It is mechanical, it can be described in one sentence, and it can be fixed with three habits that fit within the three days of the show. This article explains why context evaporates so quickly, why the useful window is shorter than people think, and how to handle a contact at the stand rather than on Sunday evening at the hotel.

What really happens between Friday and Monday

The scenario is always the same, and there is nothing unusual about it. Friday, 6 pm, the show closes. You have had around forty conversations, about ten of them genuinely serious. You get home on Saturday. On Monday, the workshop is three days behind, a customer calls about a breakdown, an urgent quote is waiting. The pile of cards sits on the corner of the desk, in plain sight — you see it every day. And it is precisely because you see it every day that it ends up becoming invisible.

What holds things up is not the follow-up itself: calling a prospect back takes three minutes. What holds things up is the task that comes before it. Before you can call, you have to copy everything out: name, company, job title, phone, email, and above all what you talked about. Forty cards means one to two hours of thankless data entry. That hour does not exist in the week after a show. So it gets pushed to the following week — where it does not exist either.

The result: by the time someone finally opens the file, all that is left is names. A video on the subject describes exactly this scene — the stand collected around a hundred contacts, "then two weeks later, someone in sales finally opens the list, and half of it is just names. No context, no idea what people wanted."

Context evaporates faster than the contact

This is the point people often grasp too late: it is not the phone number that goes out of date, it is the conversation. A technical director's name and email stay valid for years. What fades within a few days is the fact that he told you he was looking for a solution for his compressor fleet before the end of the financial year.

And it works both ways. On your side, you lose track: the third card in the pile no longer means anything to you, beyond a vague memory of a chat near the coffee stand. On his side, he visited sixty stands in two days. Your name alone is not enough to take him back to the right moment of his show. A follow-up that does not restate the context forces the prospect to do the remembering for you — and nobody does.

That is why two lines noted on the spot are worth more than a polished report written the following Tuesday. "Looking to replace a test bench, decision expected in the autumn, call back after the holidays": that sentence turns a contact into a deal. Without it, the best database in the world produces nothing but a list of names.

Forty-eight hours: the only rule that matters

On the acceptable delay, practitioners agree on a simple order of magnitude: forty-eight hours, and if possible before the show is even over. There is nothing magical about it. It comes down to competition for attention: your prospect has just met dozens of exhibitors, including several of your direct competitors. The first one to get back to him while the show is still fresh is not competing with the others: he arrives alone.

This is also what makes systems that deliver contacts after a delay so costly. A channel dedicated to trade show lead capture sums up the problem in one sentence: "a CSV file that arrives five days after the show slows you down". And a little further on: "the leads are stuck in a portal… by the time the data finally lands in your CRM, it's three days later and the conversation has already gone cold." We go through this mechanism, and what it really costs, in our article on the four ways to capture a contact at a trade show.

Let us be clear about what "following up within 48 hours" means in practice — because it does not mean sending a quote. It means sending a short message that picks up the conversation: remind them where you met, restate in one line what the person was looking for, and propose the real next step (sending documentation, a call, a visit). Three lines. The quote comes later, once the need is confirmed.

Why the pile of cards always wins

If the 48-hour rule is so well known and so rarely applied, it is because it runs into three very real obstacles in a small industrial company.

The cost of getting started is too high. Before you can follow up, you have to enter the data. A one- to two-hour task does not slip in between two emergencies: it needs a time slot, and a time slot has to be planned. As long as processing the cards is one big block, it will be postponed. The only way round it is to break it down into twenty-second actions — one contact at a time, at the stand.

The person who had the conversation is not the one who enters the data. In many small firms, the managing director runs the stand and hands the pile to the assistant on returning. She enters the contact details perfectly well — but she cannot invent the context, which has stayed in the director's head. The resulting file is clean and unusable. The context has to be captured by the person who did the talking, at the moment they did it.

Nobody measures what it costs. A contact who never gets a call makes no noise: they do not complain, they do not chase, they do not show up on any dashboard. The loss is invisible, so it is never weighed up. It is the same blind spot we describe for quotes in our article on customer follow-up: silence costs nothing at the time, and everything in the end.

A pile of business cards is not a book of prospects. It is a list of conversations that are fading, with a use-by date nobody wrote on it.

Three things to do at the stand, not afterwards

The method that works needs neither heavyweight software nor heroic discipline. It simply means moving the work: doing at the stand what you used to do once you got back.

Step 1 — record the contact straight away. The card is in your hand, the person has just left: this is the only moment when the action feels natural. Photographing the card takes two seconds and the contact is saved, clean, with phone numbers in international format. The pile no longer exists, because it never got started.

Step 2 — add one sentence of context. Right after, a note: what they are looking for, the machine or product involved, the timescale. Not a report, one sentence. That is what will make the follow-up possible, and above all comfortable — it is easy to pick up the phone when you know what to say.

Step 3 — decide on the next step before leaving the stand. Each contact gets a simple label: call back this week, put on hold, or not interested. Three categories are enough. Sorting while it is fresh is far more accurate than sorting a fortnight later through faceless names — and it cuts the volume to handle on your return by two thirds.

In the evening, there is then nothing left to enter. What remains is the real work: sending the messages on the "this week" list. One hour, once, with contacts who still remember you.

How soon should you follow up with a contact met at a trade show?

Within 48 hours, and ideally before the show itself is over. After that, the prospect has met dozens of other exhibitors and no longer links your name to a specific conversation. Your follow-up then becomes a cold prospecting message, with the response rate to match.

Why do business cards from a trade show never get processed?

Because processing them means a long data-entry job with no perceived value: copying out forty cards takes one to two hours, and that hour does not exist in the week after a show. So the pile gets put off, then forgotten. It is not a question of sales motivation but a mechanical problem of friction.

What should you note down besides the contact details?

The context: what the person was looking for, the machine or product involved, and the timescale mentioned. A contact without context is not a prospect, it is a line in a file. One sentence jotted on the back of the card, on the spot, is worth more than a detailed report written three days later.

How can you record trade show contacts without re-keying them?

By photographing each card the moment it is handed to you: a business card scanner such as Contiax extracts the contact in a few seconds and sends it to your phone's address book, a spreadsheet or straight into the CRM. The work is done at the stand, between two visitors, not on Sunday evening at the hotel.

A trade show is not won during the show. It is won in the forty-eight hours that follow, while your name still brings a specific conversation to mind for someone who had sixty of them. The stand, the hotel nights and the three days away only pay for themselves through what happens at that point — and that point cannot be bought: it has to be prepared, by removing the one thing that stops it happening, the pile.
