The real cost of a paper business card

The printer's quote is only the beginning. The reprint cycle, the dead stock at the back of the drawer and the outdated card still sitting in people's wallets cost far more than the eighty euros on the order.

Indusiax5 August 20268 min read

In almost every company we visit, there is a drawer holding business cards still in their wrap. Two packs, sometimes four. They have been there since the move, since the change of phone provider, or since the sales rep whose name is on them left. Nobody throws them away, because throwing them away would mean admitting the money was wasted.

Yet that is exactly what happened, and that is where the real cost of a business card lies. It is not the printing price. It is the ratio between what you print and what you hand out before the run goes out of date.

This article sets out a simple way to calculate that ratio for your company, and a few ways to improve it — several of which cost nothing and do not involve buying anything from anyone.

The advertised price, and why it means nothing

A run of five hundred cards on 350 gsm laminated stock costs, depending on the finish and lead time, somewhere between forty and ninety euros excluding VAT. Per card, that comes to between ten and eighteen cents. That is the figure people remember, and it is reassuring: a business card costs next to nothing.

That reasoning assumes all five hundred cards will be handed out. Let us put the question differently: how quickly do you hand out five hundred cards?

A sales rep who does four trade shows a year and around ten meetings a month might give out a hundred and fifty to two hundred a year. An SME owner who gets out less gives out forty. A technician who has some because "you have to" gives out ten. At that rate, a run of five hundred lasts three years for the sales rep, and ten years for the technician.

And hardly any card survives three years without one of its lines going out of date.

A card's expiry date

A business card carries five or six pieces of information. Each one has its own life expectancy, and it is the shortest that decides the fate of the whole run.

What is printedWhat makes it changeStability
NameNothing, unless the person leavesVery stable
Company nameTakeover, rebrandingStable
AddressMove, second siteMedium
LandlineChange of provider, new switchboardMedium
Job titlePromotion, reorganisation, wider remitLow
MobileChange of provider, switch to a company fleet planLow

In a ten-person company, it only takes one of these six lines to change for that person's run to become unusable. And across ten people, several such changes happen every year: a promotion, a new mobile, a new site opening.

So the honest calculation is not "how much does a card cost" but "how many cards do we hand out between two reprints". On a run of five hundred, of which a hundred and twenty were given out before a change made the rest obsolete, the real cost of a card actually handed to someone is not fifteen cents: it is sixty.

Dead stock: what it really costs

Faced with stock that has gone out of date, people react in one of three ways, and none of them is right.

Crossing it out. You strike through the old number and write the new one in pen. It works — except that the card you hand over is the first object the other person associates with your company. A hand-corrected card says "we didn't take the time", at the very moment you are trying to prove the opposite. In a market where you are selling rigour, that is a poor trade-off.

Using them up. You keep handing out the old ones "for now", mentioning that the number has changed. Half the people will have forgotten before they get home.

The drawer. You put them away, order new ones, and throw nothing out. This is the most common reaction, and the most expensive: the money is lost in full, but because the expense was already booked last year, it appears nowhere in this year's decision. You reprint five hundred cards thinking it costs eighty euros, when it actually costs eighty euros plus the sixty already buried.

The cost you never see: the outdated card

Everything above can be put into figures. The real cost cannot — and it is the one that should drive the decision.

The cards you handed out before the change are not in your drawer. They are in wallets, desk drawers and card holders, with people who met you once and thought your business looked sound. Some of them will call you in six months, when the need arises.

They will dial a number that no longer answers. And you will never know.

That is where the real expense lies: not in the boxes thrown away, but in the deal that never came back to you because the only link left between that prospect and you was a piece of card printed two years earlier. In a small or mid-sized industrial company where an order runs into thousands of euros, a single missed call wipes out ten years of printing budget.

The cost of a business card is not measured by the price of the paper. It is measured by the number of people who, one day, wanted to reach you and could not.

Work out your own in five minutes

The method comes down to four figures, all easy to find.

1. The amount on your last printing quote.
2. The number of cards from that run actually handed out — an honest estimate is enough: look at what is left in the box.
3. The time spent gathering the details, approving the print proof and chasing the printer. Cost it at the hourly rate of whoever did it, managing director included.
4. The value of the previous stock thrown away or put in a drawer.

Add 1, 3 and 4, and divide by 2. The result is what each card handed to someone really costs you. In the cases we have seen, it falls between forty cents and one euro fifty — three to ten times the unit price on the quote.

What this does not mean

This is not a case for getting rid of paper. Handing over a card is a gesture, a moment of physical contact in a conversation, and nothing quite replaces it: at an industrial trade show, a card is expected, and not having one comes across as offhand.

The issue is not paper. It is the reprint cycle, and there are several ways to lengthen it without buying anything from anyone:

Print shorter runs. Two hundred cards cost only slightly less than five hundred — so noticeably more per card — but they run out before they go out of date. The extra unit cost is far lower than the cost of a stock that has to be scrapped.

Do not print what changes. A precise job title and a direct mobile number are the two most fragile lines. Many companies stick to the name, the company, the landline and the email address — and give the mobile number verbally to those who need it.

Put what changes somewhere else. A QR code or a chip on the back of the card points to a page you can correct in thirty seconds. The card stays the same; it is the contact details that become editable. That is what smart business cards do, and it is their only serious argument — the rest, the novelty effect on a stand, wears off within a season.
